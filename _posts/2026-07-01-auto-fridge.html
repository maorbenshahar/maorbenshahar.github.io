Fix a mathematical language, some known results, and an interesting unproved theorem. Resolve the possible constructions into small, equally expensive steps. What mathematical properties of this network govern the growth of knowledge and the cost of finding a proof?

The hypergraph

Let \(V\) be the countable set of theorems, definitions, types, and intermediate constructions, including their local contexts. A rule \(P\to v\) describes one elementary way to construct a node \(v\) from all the premises in a set \(P\). We call such a rule a directed hyperedge because it can require several premises together. Write \(E\) for the collection of these rules. Assuming at most two premises per rule, our hypergraph is

\[ \mathcal H=(V,E),\qquad E\subseteq\{(P,v):P\subseteq V,\ |P|\le2,\ v\in V\}. \]

Alternative edges into \(v\) represent alternative constructions. We fix the foundation, available operations, and step resolution; binary prerequisites and unit costs are model assumptions.

A run begins with a fixed finite core \(K_0\) of axioms and library results, and retains what it constructs. Every later known node must have a derivation from that core. Write \(K\) for its current library and \(K_t\) for that library after \(t\) proposals. A successful construction adds its conclusion:

\[ K\longmapsto K\cup\{v\} \quad\text{when }(P,v)\in E\text{ and }P\subseteq K. \]

Call a finite library admissible if some sequence of these steps builds it from \(K_0\). This requires more than a connection drawn back to the core: every premise of a rule must already be available when its conclusion is added. Randomly marking nodes as known does not ensure this. A statistical model can instead choose a distribution over valid histories, which induces a distribution over their resulting libraries.

This models small proof-assistant steps. Writing a tactic inline reaches an intermediate node; naming a lemma records a construction for reuse. We assume useful constructions are retained either way. Tactics themselves can belong to the library. Choosing and proposing a step costs one unit; checking costs are neglected. Failed proposals also cost one unit and leave \(K\) unchanged. The full hypergraph is an idealization, not a map supplied to the prover.

For example, from a hypothesis \(h:A\land B\), we can extract a proof of \(A\), extract a proof of \(B\), and combine them to prove \(B\land A\). Counting each extraction and combination as one step gives

\[ h\to a:A,\qquad h\to b:B,\qquad \{a,b\}\to g:B\land A. \]

The two extractions can be taken in either order, and their results remain available.

Closure and combining libraries

Starting from a library \(K\), let \(F(K)\) add every construction whose premises are already known. Repeating this operation gives everything obtainable from \(K\), its closure. Writing \(F^n\) for \(n\) iterations, with \(F^0(K)=K\), we have

\[ F(K)=K\cup\{v:\exists(P,v)\in E,\ P\subseteq K\}, \qquad \operatorname{cl}(K)=\bigcup_{n\ge0}F^n(K). \]

These parallel iterations do not measure serial proof cost. Closure contains the starting library, respects inclusion, and adds nothing further when applied a second time:

\[ \begin{gathered} K\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(K),\qquad K\subseteq L\Rightarrow\operatorname{cl}(K)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(L),\\ \operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(K))=\operatorname{cl}(K),\\ v\in\operatorname{cl}(K) \iff v\text{ has a finite derivation from }K. \end{gathered} \]

Proof. The first two follow by induction on iterations of \(F\). Each rule has finitely many premises, so premises in the union all occur by some finite iteration; the union is therefore closed. Inductively expanding a construction gives a finite derivation, and induction along any finite derivation puts its conclusion in the union. The union of known sets along any schedule that eventually applies every enabled rule is therefore \(\operatorname{cl}(K)\).

Along a run, \(K_0\subseteq K_t\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(K_0)\), hence

\[ \operatorname{cl}(K_t)=\operatorname{cl}(K_0). \]

The constructed library grows within a fixed deductive closure.

Two admissible libraries can be combined: first build one, then replay the history of the other, skipping results already present. Their union is still admissible and has the same full closure. What sharing can change is which constructions are available next:

\[ F(A)\cup F(B)\subseteq F(A\cup B). \]

This inclusion follows from monotonicity and can be strict. For example, take a common core \(K_0=\{c\}\) and the rules

\[ c\to a,\qquad c\to b,\qquad \{a,b\}\to G. \]

The libraries \(A=\{c,a\}\) and \(B=\{c,b\}\) each take one construction to acquire. From either alone, proving \(G\) takes two more steps: construct the missing premise, then combine them. From their union it takes one. In terms of parallel expansion,

\[ F(A)=F(B)=\{c,a,b\},\qquad F(A\cup B)=\{c,a,b,G\}. \]

All three libraries have the same eventual consequences. In this example, sharing reduces the work still needed to obtain the target.

Construction distance

Discoveries leave deductive closure unchanged, but can shorten the remaining construction of a target. Fix a target \(G\) derivable from the initial library \(K_0\). For a current library \(K\supseteq K_0\), define its construction distance \(d(K,G)\) as the fewest further steps needed to obtain \(G\):

\[ d(K,G)=\min\{m:G\text{ can be added to }K \text{ in }m\text{ elementary steps}\}. \]

Set \(d(K,G)=0\) when \(G\in K\). A derivation may share previously constructed premises between several branches. Consequently this counts constructions in the hypergraph, including that reuse. For \(K\subseteq L\), and for a single allowed addition \(K'=K\cup\{v\}\),

\[ \boxed{d(L,G)\le d(K,G),\qquad 0\le d(K,G)-d(K',G)\le1.} \]

Proof. Extra knowledge can be ignored, so it cannot increase the minimum. Conversely, prepend the step \(K\to K'\) to a shortest derivation from \(K'\): \(d(K,G)\le1+d(K',G)\).

Thus unit steps partition into those that decrease this distance by one and those that leave it unchanged. Let \(\tau\) count the proposals up to the first proof of \(G\), so \(\tau=\inf\{t:G\in K_t\}\). A successful run satisfies the exact identity

\[ \tau=d(K_0,G)+\#\{\text{steps that leave }d\text{ unchanged}\}. \]

There may be many such steps, even when every construction is valid and retained. This minimum belongs to the fixed hypergraph and step resolution; computing it already requires knowledge of possible proofs. A step that leaves it unchanged can still help discover a route, or serve other targets.

Finite closure bound. If \(\operatorname{cl}(K_0)\) has \(N\) nodes, a process that keeps adding new enabled nodes reaches \(G\) in at most \(N-|K_0|\) additions. Indeed, before \(G\) is known, the first missing step of a derivation of \(G\) supplies an enabled addition. This bounds successful constructions; locating them may require failed proposals.

Library size does not determine remaining work

Consider two chains of elementary constructions starting from the same core node \(c\). Each chain has length \(L\), and the target \(G\) ends the first:

\[ \begin{aligned} c&\to b_1\to b_2\to\cdots\to b_L=G,\\ c&\to u_1\to u_2\to\cdots\to u_L. \end{aligned} \]

There are no other rules. For \(0<m<L\), compare the library \(K_b=\{c,b_1,\ldots,b_m\}\) with \(K_u=\{c,u_1,\ldots,u_m\}\). Both contain \(m+1\) nodes, both took \(m\) constructions to build, and both are connected to the core by valid proofs. Nevertheless,

\[ d(K_b,G)=L-m,\qquad d(K_u,G)=L. \]

Proof. The only way to obtain \(G\) is to finish its chain. The first library has already completed \(m\) of those constructions; the second has completed none. Equal amounts of acquired knowledge can leave very different work for a given target.

What does the four-node formula with \(\rho\) calculate?

This is a different initial-condition model, which does not ensure a history from our fixed core. Divide nodes into groups of four; within each group, any two nodes can produce either remaining node. Now suppose each node is independently supplied as known with probability \(\rho\).

Fix one node. It is eventually known in two disjoint cases: it was supplied initially, with probability \(\rho\); or it was not, with probability \(1-\rho\), and at least two of the other three were supplied. Exactly two can be chosen in three ways, giving \(3\rho^2(1-\rho)\); all three have probability \(\rho^3\). Thus its probability of becoming known is

\[ \begin{aligned} x_{\mathrm{groups}}(\rho) &=\rho+(1-\rho)\bigl[3\rho^2(1-\rho)+\rho^3\bigr]\\ &=\rho+(1-\rho)(3\rho^2-2\rho^3). \end{aligned} \]

For example, \(\rho=1/2\) gives \(x_{\mathrm{groups}}=3/4\). By symmetry this is also the expected fraction known. The calculation is exact under independent supply, but it does not establish a law for libraries produced by a prover. For those, the distribution of known nodes must come from construction histories.

Percolation and finite observations

Percolation can also start from a fixed core: explore outward from a chosen origin along available edges. Every discovered node is then connected to that origin. Its phase transition varies the random availability of edges across models. It does not come from repeatedly deriving consequences in one fixed, fully available graph.

Our closure operation is related to directed-hypergraph propagation, with all premises of a rule required together. Relating a percolation parameter to a prover would need a model of which constructions it can discover. We have not supplied such a model.

For comparison, ordinary bond percolation keeps each edge of a graph independently with probability \(p\). Suppose the graph \(\Gamma\) is infinite, connected, locally finite, and vertex-transitive. Fix a starting vertex \(o\), and let \(B_R\) contain the vertices within distance \(R\) of it. We compare the probability \(q_R(p)\) of an open path leaving this finite ball with the probability \(\theta(p)\) of connection to infinity:

\[ q_R(p)=\Pr_p(o\text{ connects to the boundary of }B_R), \qquad \theta(p)=\Pr_p(o\leftrightarrow\infty). \]

Reaching the boundary means crossing an edge leaving the ball. The critical probability \(p_c=\inf\{p:\theta(p)>0\}\) marks the onset of a positive chance of connection to infinity. The sharpness bounds hold with some \(c=c(p,\Gamma)>0\):

\[ \begin{array}{ll} p<p_c:&q_R(p)\le e^{-cR},\\ p>p_c:&0\le q_R(p)-\theta(p)\le e^{-cR}. \end{array} \]

The supercritical bound is Diskin and collaborators, Theorem 1 (2026); the subcritical bound is classical. These concern complete finite balls of a specified random graph. Applying them to proof hypergraphs would require a corresponding theorem: countability and equal costs do not supply independence, symmetry, or ordinary connectivity. An arbitrary prover trace need not expose a complete ball. Sharpness also does not mean the order parameter must jump discontinuously.

Magma equations as a testing ground

A magma is a set with one binary operation \(\star\), with no laws assumed initially. An equational law is an identity required to hold for every assignment of its variables, such as associativity, \((x\star y)\star z=x\star(y\star z)\). A proof task fixes some laws \(T\) as assumptions and asks whether another equation follows in every magma satisfying \(T\).

This gives us a controlled setting for algebraic proof search. Terms have a simple syntax, but the prover must still choose substitutions, rewrite locations, and intermediate equations. Equational reasoning is complete for these positive implication questions: every valid implication has a finite derivation using equality rules and substitution. The Equational Theories Project paper explains this connection and why short equations can require difficult proofs.

The project's corpus already classifies all 22,028,942 non-reflexive implication questions among 4,694 laws with at most four operation occurrences, with Lean-checked proofs or refutations. This gives evaluation answers without giving us every proof route or its minimum cost. The published implication edges usually summarize whole proofs; they are not our elementary hyperedges.

A first experiment could fix \(T\), choose known positive implications as targets, and compare provers with explicitly restricted libraries. Every added lemma must come with a derivation from the same \(T\); importing an arbitrary magma law would change the problem. Library variants can be prefixes of recorded valid runs. Measure library construction and subsequent target search separately, counting failed proposals as well as the final proof length. The project's Vampire workflow provides a starting point for classical automation and Lean reconstruction.

This would test rewriting, guidance, and lemma reuse. It would not by itself establish statistical laws for mathematics generally: the experiment fixes the language and types, and positive equational proving does not test all the choices involved in developing new definitions or constructing counterexamples. Generalization would require comparisons with other domains. No prover runs for this experiment are reported here.

A finite sequence of graphs that preserves the fixed core

Fix an elementary equational calculus. Bound term size and the available variable names to obtain finite equation sets \(V_n\), increasing to cover every equation. Keep the inference edges entirely within each set, giving nested finite hypergraphs \(\mathcal H_n\). Begin after the core, target, and full history of the starting library \(K\) all fit.

Let \(d_n(K,G)\) be the shortest construction within \(\mathcal H_n\), or infinity if there is none. Enlarging the graph preserves old routes, so

\[ d_{n+1}(K,G)\le d_n(K,G). \]

If the unrestricted minimum \(d(K,G)\) is finite, an optimal proof uses finitely many equations and eventually fits inside one \(\mathcal H_n\). From that point onward, \(d_n(K,G)=d(K,G)\). This proves eventual agreement, without telling us when that point has been reached. Small finite graphs could therefore support exact comparisons with search policies.

For fixed \(K,G\), this minimum eventually stops changing. To study nontrivial scaling, we would need a growing family of targets, or a specified policy whose search effort is measured as more options become available. Whether such experiments exhibit thresholds remains a research question.

What might a prover exploit?

The graph supplies joint prerequisites, shared intermediates, and dependency bottlenecks. A prover must select steps without knowing their full consequences. Humans and autoprovers nevertheless seem to have a sense of direction: we simplify a goal, introduce a construction, or choose a promising lemma before possessing the completed proof.

The distance above gives one precise interpretation. Let \(p_t\) be the conditional probability that the next proposal decreases \(d(K_t,G)\), given the entire run so far. If \(p_t\ge\varepsilon>0\) until success, then

\[ \mathbb E[\tau]\le\frac{d(K_0,G)}{\varepsilon}. \]

Proof. With \(T_n=\min(\tau,n)\), expected accumulated decreases are at least \(\varepsilon\mathbb E[T_n]\) and at most \(d(K_0,G)\). Let \(n\to\infty\). No independence of successive choices is needed. For uniform selection from a finite candidate set, \(p_t\) is simply its fraction of distance-decreasing moves.

Can recognizable features of the hypergraph predict those moves? Does useful direction come from simulating short continuations, learned structural regularities, or both? Can properties measured along valid construction histories predict the cost of reaching other targets? These are research questions; the formulas above do not supply that prediction.

The refrigerator analogy asks for a resource law for reaching a desired state. We have specified a unit of reasoning cost and exhibited structural and statistical laws. Relating them to physical work or a mathematical notion of entropy remains to be done.
