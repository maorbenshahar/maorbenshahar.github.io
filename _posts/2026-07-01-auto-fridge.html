--- layout: post title: "An autoprover as a refrigerator (DRAFT)" date: 2026-06-18 summary: "Work, mathematical progress, and a model with a threshold in prover efficiency." tags: "#lean #math #AI #statistical-mechanics" ---

To make a block of ice in a warm room, a refrigerator must spend work. The amount depends on both the ice we want and the refrigerator's efficiency. We would like a similar account of an autoprover: starting from a known library, how much work does it need to reach an interesting theorem?

We will take the theorem's interest as given and study the work needed to prove it. A simple tree model lets us calculate how this work depends on two things: the number of possible continuations and the prover's ability to choose among them. Improving that ability produces a phase transition in the model. Whether a similar transition occurs in mathematical proof search remains a research question.

Building the library

Fix a mathematical language, a finite starting library \(K_0\), and a target theorem \(G\) that follows from it. The prover builds its library one verified construction at a time. We assign each step one unit of work, including deciding what to construct and verifying it.

A construction may require several known results together: for example, \(a=b\) and \(b=c\) allow us to prove \(a=c\). We represent such a construction by a directed hyperedge \(P\to v\), where \(P\) is its finite set of premises and \(v\) is its conclusion. The nodes can also be definitions, types, tactics, or intermediate judgments; each records any assumptions under which it is valid. There can be several edges leading to the same node, representing different ways to construct it.

Write \(K_t\) for the library after \(t\) steps. A step uses an edge whose premises are in \(K_t\) and adds its new conclusion:

\[ K_{t+1}:=K_t\cup\{v\},\qquad P\subseteq K_t,\quad v\notin K_t. \]

We assume the prover remembers and can reuse its constructions, whether written inline or saved as named lemmas. Every known node therefore has a construction history from \(K_0\). All nodes obtainable by finite such histories form the closure of \(K_0\), which we assume is countable. The prover explores this graph without being given its complete structure.

How does working backward from a goal fit?

Suppose we know \(n=6m\) for integers \(n,m\), and want to prove that \(n^2\) is even. Working backward, the prover first establishes that it would suffice to prove \(n\) even: if \(n=2k\) for some integer \(k\), then \(n^2=2(2k^2)\). This verifies a conditional lemma, which it can store and reuse. The remaining goal is to prove \(n\) even. Since \(n=2(3m)\), that goal follows, and the lemma completes the original proof.

Working backward chooses which intermediate claims to pursue. The library still grows through verified constructions, including any conditional lemmas discovered along the way. Establishing such a lemma may itself take several steps.

Distance and efficiency

The construction distance \(d(K_0,G)\) is the fewest steps needed to add \(G\) to the starting library. It counts the intermediate constructions needed for a proof, allowing them to be reused. A prover can take longer because it also constructs results that do not help reach \(G\).

For example, suppose the shortest construction takes 20 steps, but a prover averages 2,000 steps before reaching the target. We would call its efficiency \(20/2000=1\%\). To express this generally, let \(\pi\) denote the prover and \(\tau_{\pi,G}\) the number of steps up to and including its first construction of \(G\). Its mean work \(W_\pi(G)\) and efficiency \(\eta_\pi(G)\) are

\[ W_\pi(G)=\mathbb E[\tau_{\pi,G}],\qquad \eta_\pi(G)=\frac{d(K_0,G)}{W_\pi(G)}. \]

The expectation averages over repeated runs. We consider provers that continue constructing until they reach the target; if a run never reaches it, set \(\tau_{\pi,G}=\infty\). For an initially unknown target and finite mean work, efficiency lies between zero and one, since every run reaching \(G\) must use at least the minimum number of constructions.

This definition separates the length of a shortest proof from the work spent finding it. It does not yet predict the work. For that we need assumptions about the graph and the prover's choices.

A model of guidance

We begin with a tree: every node has \(b\ge2\) children until depth \(L\ge1\), where depth counts edges from the root. The root is already known, and each edge constructs its child from its parent. The terminal nodes are called leaves. Choose the target uniformly among the leaves; each has construction distance exactly \(L\). This is a special case of our hypergraph, with one premise per edge.

To model the prover's judgment, give it a guide that marks promising edges. The guide marks every edge on the path to the target. It independently marks each other edge with probability \(q\). Thus \(q\) measures how often the guide retains an unhelpful continuation. At \(q=1\), everything is marked and the guide tells us nothing. At \(q=0\), only the correct path is marked. Varying \(q\) changes the guidance while keeping the graph and target distribution fixed.

The target statement is given to the prover; its route through the tree is unknown. The guide's marks are chosen once. Constructing a node reveals the marks on its outgoing edges; the root's marks are available initially. The prover recognizes the target when it constructs it, but receives no clues about its location beyond these marks. Unexplored leaves cannot be tested for free. We include using the guide in the unit step cost and assume its training is already complete when the run begins.

The prover follows one marked branch until it reaches the target or the branch ends. If it ends elsewhere, the prover returns to the nearest earlier node with an unexplored marked child and continues from there. This is depth-first search. At each node, child order is chosen uniformly at random, independently of the guide and of other orders. All constructed nodes remain in the library; returning to them adds no new construction. The calculation below averages over target choices, guide marks, and search order.

Calculating the work

Before the terminal depth, a node away from the target path has \(b\) possible children, each marked with probability \(q\). Its mean number of marked children is therefore \(\mu=bq\). A node on the target path has one correct child and \(b-1\) others, so its mean number of marked children leading elsewhere is \(a=(b-1)q\).

Consider one of these detours. Starting with its first node, successive generations contain \(1,\mu,\mu^2,\ldots\) nodes on average. The prover explores that detour before reaching the target exactly when its branch is ordered before the correct branch. This has probability \(1/2\), regardless of how many other children there are.

Now sum over all departure points along the \(L\)-step target path. A generation \(r\) edges below the first node of a detour fits at \(L-r\) departure points. Each contributes an expected \(a\mu^r/2\) constructions before the target. Adding the \(L\) constructions on the target path gives the mean work, which we denote by \(W_L(q)\):

\[ \boxed{ W_L(q)=L+\frac a2\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}(L-r)\mu^r, \qquad \mu=bq,\quad a=(b-1)q. } \]

The first term is the shortest proof length. The second is the work spent constructing other valid results. Its growth depends on whether detours shrink or expand from one generation to the next:

Mean marked children, \(\mu\)Mean work as \(L\to\infty\)Efficiency \(L/W_L\)
\(\mu<1\)\(\displaystyle \left(1+\frac{a}{2(1-\mu)}\right)L+O(1)\)Positive limit
\(\mu=1\)\(\displaystyle L+\frac a4L(L+1)\)Decays as \(1/L\)
\(\mu>1\)\(\Theta(\mu^L)\)Decays exponentially, up to a factor \(L\)

Dividing the shortest proof length \(L\) by this mean work gives the efficiency. Its limit as the targets become more distant is

\[ \eta_\infty(q)= \begin{cases} \displaystyle\left(1+\frac{(b-1)q}{2(1-bq)}\right)^{-1},&q<1/b,\\[6pt] 0,&q\ge1/b. \end{cases} \]

For every finite depth, efficiency varies smoothly with \(q\). The limit has a threshold at \(q_c=1/b\): efficiency rises continuously from zero as the guide improves past it. The unguided prover, with \(q=1\), eventually succeeds on each finite tree, but its mean work grows exponentially with proof distance.

Evaluating the sum and the transition window

Summing the geometric series and its derivative gives

\[ \sum_{r=0}^{L-1}(L-r)\mu^r= \begin{cases} \displaystyle\frac{\mu^{L+1}-(L+1)\mu+L}{(\mu-1)^2},&\mu\ne1,\\[6pt] L(L+1)/2,&\mu=1. \end{cases} \]

Substituting this into the work formula proves the three growth rates in the table. To see how a finite tree approaches the transition, set \(\mu=1+s/L\) for fixed \(s\). Divide the sum by \(L^2\) and take \(L\to\infty\). The resulting Riemann sum converges to

\[ \int_0^1(1-x)e^{sx}\,dx= \frac{e^s-1-s}{s^2}, \]

with value \(1/2\) at \(s=0\). This describes the crossover within a window of width proportional to \(1/L\) around \(\mu=1\).

Do typical runs follow the mean?

The mean alone could hide unusually expensive runs. Here we can also prove a limit for individual runs, by constructing all depths as truncations of one infinite tree with a marked target path.

At path level \(j\), let \(X_j(h)\) count the detour nodes explored before the correct child, including immediate children and at most \(h\) generations below them. As \(h\) increases, these counts increase to a limit \(X_j\). The limits are independent and identically distributed across path levels, and the generation count above gives

\[ \mathbb E[X_j(h)]=\frac a2\sum_{r=0}^{h}\mu^r. \]

Thus \(\mathbb E[X_j]=a/[2(1-\mu)]\) below threshold, and is infinite at or above it. A run to depth \(L\) costs \(\tau_L=L+\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}X_j(L-j-1)\). For fixed \(h\), all but the last \(h\) terms of this sum are at least \(X_j(h)\). Apply the law of large numbers to these truncated counts, then let \(h\to\infty\). Below threshold, the untruncated counts \(X_j\) provide a matching upper bound. Hence, almost surely,

\[ \frac{\tau_L}{L}\longrightarrow \begin{cases} \displaystyle1+\frac a{2(1-\mu)},&\mu<1,\\[5pt] \infty,&\mu\ge1. \end{cases} \]

Below threshold, choosing a budget \(cL\) with \(c\) greater than the finite limit gives success probability tending to one. At or above threshold, every fixed multiplier \(c\) gives success probability tending to zero. This statement concerns the depth-first prover defined above.

Could a better search order remove the exponential cost?

Let \(M_L\) count the leaves whose entire paths are marked. Even if a prover receives every mark for free, the target is equally likely to be any of these leaves. Indeed, suppose the full tree has \(N\) edges, of which \(m\) are marked. For each compatible target, its \(L\) path edges are automatically marked, leaving the same probability for the other marks:

\[ q^{m-L}(1-q)^{N-m}. \]

The initially uniform target distribution therefore remains uniform among the compatible leaves. At \(q=0\) or \(q=1\), this follows directly. Our information assumptions give the prover no further clues.

Constructing a leaf either reaches the target or rules out that location. Even if constructing prerequisites were free, finding a uniformly placed target among \(M_L\) candidates would require at least \((M_L+1)/2\) leaf constructions on average. The earlier generation count gives \(\mathbb E M_L=1+a\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}\mu^r\). Every search algorithm under these assumptions therefore satisfies

\[ \mathbb E\tau_L\ge \max\left\{L,\;1+\frac a2\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}\mu^r\right\}. \]

Write \(W_L^*(q)\) for the infimum of mean work over algorithms chosen before the random target and guide are sampled. The lower bound above and the achievable depth-first cost together show that

\[ \lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{\log W_L^*(q)}{L} =\max\{0,\log(bq)\},\qquad q>0. \]

Optimal mean work is therefore linear below threshold and exponential above it. At the threshold, these bounds leave a gap between linear and quadratic. The quadratic cost in the table is proved for our depth-first algorithm; we have not established the optimal critical cost.

Allowing different graph shapes

The threshold involves both the growth of the graph, through \(b\), and the quality of the guide, through \(q\). More possible continuations require stronger guidance. We can extend the calculation to graphs in which different kinds of branch produce different numbers of descendants.

Let \(B_{ij}\) be the mean number of possible children of kind \(j\) at a node of kind \(i\). Given those possible children, suppose the guide independently marks each with probability \(Q_{ij}\). The mean number of marked children is then \(M_{ij}=B_{ij}Q_{ij}\). Multiplying by the matrix \(M\) propagates expected counts to the next generation. Under the conditions below, its largest eigenvalue replaces \(bq\) as the parameter controlling the transition.

The matrix calculation and its assumptions

Assume different branches have disjoint descendants and reproduce independently according to their kind. Keep the same depth-first policy, with uniform child orders independent of the descendants. At each target-path node, let the same nonzero row vector \(a\) give the expected numbers of marked children leading away from the target, separated by kind. Then \(aM^r\mathbf1\) is the expected total in detour generation \(r\), where \(\mathbf1\) is a column vector of ones. The same counting argument gives

\[ W_L=L+\frac12\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}(L-r)\,aM^r\mathbf1. \]

Assume also that \(M\) is primitive: some power has all entries positive. Perron–Frobenius theory then gives \(aM^r\mathbf1\sim c\gamma^r\), where \(\gamma\) is the largest eigenvalue and \(c>0\). Inserting this growth into the sum proves linear, quadratic, or exponential mean work according as \(\gamma<1\), \(\gamma=1\), or \(\gamma>1\). Below threshold, summing the matrix geometric series gives \(W_L/L\to1+\tfrac12a(I-M)^{-1}\mathbf1\), where \(I\) is the identity matrix.

The primitivity assumption affects the critical behavior. For example, with \(a=(1,0)\) and \(M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), we have \(aM^r\mathbf1=r+1\). Substitution gives \(W_L=L+L(L+1)(L+2)/12\): cubic cost, although the largest eigenvalue is one. This matrix is not primitive.

The connection to percolation is the thinning of possible continuations: away from the target, the marked branches form a branching process whose mean reproduction controls their growth. Boczkowski, Feige, Korman, and Rodeh prove search transitions with a different model of noisy guidance. Our calculation above is for the specific marking rule given here.

Relation to the recent sharpness theorem

The supercritical sharpness theorem concerns independent bond percolation on infinite transitive graphs. It does not give a work bound for proof search. Applying such a result here would require a model linking its random connections to the constructions a prover explores.

Measuring work and progress

To compare actual provers, fix the starting library and targets, then record each run's library additions and when it reaches its target. Their count measures work in our model. Recording computation costs alongside them would let us test the equal-cost assumption.

For a fixed prover \(\pi\) and theorem \(G\), let \(F_{\pi,G}(B)\) be the probability of reaching \(G\) within \(B\) constructions:

\[ F_{\pi,G}(B)=\Pr(\tau_{\pi,G}\le B). \]

Repeated runs estimate this curve. For example, the smallest budget at which it reaches \(0.9\) is the work allowance needed for a 90% chance of proving \(G\). An unfinished run stopped at budget \(B\) tells us that more than \(B\) steps were needed, but not how many more. Estimating the full mean work therefore requires information about longer runs too.

How does the success curve determine mean work?

A run requiring \(\tau\) constructions contributes one unit for each integer \(t\) with \(0\le t<\tau\). Averaging this count gives

\[ W_\pi(G)=\sum_{t\ge0}\Pr(\tau_{\pi,G}>t) =\sum_{t\ge0}[1-F_{\pi,G}(t)]. \]

The formula also allows an infinite mean. Observations restricted to a finite budget determine only a finite part of the sum.

How could we measure progress toward several theorems?

Choose a finite set of desired theorems, denoted \(\mathcal T\), none already in the starting library. Assign each theorem \(G\) a nonnegative score \(w_G\) expressing how much we want that result. The progress score \(U(K)\) of a library \(K\) adds the scores of targets it contains:

\[ U(K)=\sum_{G\in\mathcal T\cap K}w_G. \]

The condition \(G\in\mathcal T\cap K\) means that \(G\) is both a chosen target and already proved. For example, if targets \(A\) and \(B\) have scores 2 and 5, proving only \(A\) gives score 2; proving both gives 7. Setting every score to one simply counts how many targets have been proved.

We can now ask for enough work to reach a desired score \(u\) with probability at least \(\alpha\). Write \(K_\pi[B]\) for the library obtained by prover \(\pi\) with a budget of at most \(B\) constructions, including runs that finish earlier. The required budget is

\[ C_\pi(u,\alpha)=\inf\{B:\Pr[U(K_\pi[B])\ge u]\ge\alpha\}. \]

This selects the smallest budget meeting the requested score and reliability, or infinity if no budget does. Both the targets and their scores are supplied as part of the research objective.

Minimum construction distance is harder to measure: a discovered proof gives an upper bound on it, but may not be shortest. On small graphs we could compute the minimum and measure efficiency directly. On larger instances, two provers on the same target can still be compared through their work ratio, since the unknown distance cancels.

Magma equations offer a controlled testing ground. Terms use one binary operation, while proofs require choices of substitutions, rewrites, and intermediate equations. The Equational Theories Project classifies implication questions among 4,694 short laws using Lean-checked generating proofs and refutations, extended by transitivity and duality. Its positive implications provide targets. To study our graph, we would need their elementary proof steps and a record of everything else constructed during search. Each run's lemmas must follow from its chosen starting assumptions.

What would the refrigerator analogy tell us?

In the tree model, the target's distance \(L\) and the graph's branching \(b\) describe the task, while \(q\) describes the guide. Together they predict the work. Every target has the same shortest proof length, yet the work needed to find it can be linear or exponential depending on guidance. This gives us a resource law for a specified family of tasks, although it does not assign a temperature to an individual theorem.

For actual autoprovers, the next question is whether measured graph growth and guidance predict the observed work curves. Testing a phase transition would require growing target families, rather than a sharp improvement on one finite benchmark. Shared lemmas, branches that merge, and constructions requiring several premises may change the answer. Extending the calculation to those features is the next mathematical problem; testing it on prover runs is the next empirical one.
