Fix a mathematical language, some known results, and an interesting unproved theorem. Resolve the possible constructions into small, equally expensive steps. What mathematical properties of this network govern the growth of knowledge and the cost of finding a proof?

The hypergraph

Let \(V\) be the countable set of theorems, definitions, types, and intermediate constructions, including their local contexts. A rule \(P\to v\) describes one elementary way to construct a node \(v\) from all the premises in a set \(P\). We call such a rule a directed hyperedge because it can require several premises together. Write \(E\) for the collection of these rules. Assuming at most two premises per rule, our hypergraph is

\[ \mathcal H=(V,E),\qquad E\subseteq\{(P,v):P\subseteq V,\ |P|\le2,\ v\in V\}. \]

Alternative edges into \(v\) represent alternative constructions. We fix the foundation, available operations, and step resolution; binary prerequisites and unit costs are model assumptions.

A run begins with a finite library \(K_0\) and retains what it constructs. Write \(K\) for its current library and \(K_t\) for that library after \(t\) proposals. A successful construction adds its conclusion:

\[ K\longmapsto K\cup\{v\} \quad\text{when }(P,v)\in E\text{ and }P\subseteq K. \]

This models small proof-assistant steps. Writing a tactic inline reaches an intermediate node; naming a lemma records a construction for reuse. We assume useful constructions are retained either way. Tactics themselves can belong to the library. Choosing and proposing a step costs one unit; checking costs are neglected. Failed proposals also cost one unit and leave \(K\) unchanged. The full hypergraph is an idealization, not a map supplied to the prover.

For example, from a hypothesis \(h:A\land B\), we can extract a proof of \(A\), extract a proof of \(B\), and combine them to prove \(B\land A\). Counting each extraction and combination as one step gives

\[ h\to a:A,\qquad h\to b:B,\qquad \{a,b\}\to g:B\land A. \]

The two extractions can be taken in either order, and their results remain available.

Closure and combining libraries

Starting from a library \(K\), let \(F(K)\) add every construction whose premises are already known. Repeating this operation gives everything obtainable from \(K\), its closure. Writing \(F^n\) for \(n\) iterations, with \(F^0(K)=K\), we have

\[ F(K)=K\cup\{v:\exists(P,v)\in E,\ P\subseteq K\}, \qquad \operatorname{cl}(K)=\bigcup_{n\ge0}F^n(K). \]

These parallel iterations do not measure serial proof cost. Closure contains the starting library, respects inclusion, and adds nothing further when applied a second time:

\[ \begin{gathered} K\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(K),\qquad K\subseteq L\Rightarrow\operatorname{cl}(K)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(L),\\ \operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(K))=\operatorname{cl}(K),\\ v\in\operatorname{cl}(K) \iff v\text{ has a finite derivation from }K. \end{gathered} \]

Proof. The first two follow by induction on iterations of \(F\). Each rule has finitely many premises, so premises in the union all occur by some finite iteration; the union is therefore closed. Inductively expanding a construction gives a finite derivation, and induction along any finite derivation puts its conclusion in the union. The union of known sets along any schedule that eventually applies every enabled rule is therefore \(\operatorname{cl}(K)\).

Along a run, \(K_0\subseteq K_t\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(K_0)\), hence

\[ \operatorname{cl}(K_t)=\operatorname{cl}(K_0). \]

The constructed library grows within a fixed deductive closure.

Combining libraries can produce more than their separate consequences:

\[ \operatorname{cl}(A)\cup\operatorname{cl}(B) \subseteq\operatorname{cl}(A\cup B). \]

The inclusion follows from monotonicity. With at most one premise per rule it is equality: each derivation follows a chain from one library (or a rule with no premises). Binary rules allow strict inclusion: with the sole rule \(\{a,b\}\to c\), neither \(\{a\}\) nor \(\{b\}\) yields \(c\), but their union does. This is a minimal model of two bodies of mathematics becoming useful together.

Construction distance

Discoveries leave deductive closure unchanged, but can shorten the remaining construction of a target. Fix a target \(G\) derivable from the initial library \(K_0\). For a current library \(K\supseteq K_0\), define its construction distance \(d(K,G)\) as the fewest further steps needed to obtain \(G\):

\[ d(K,G)=\min\{m:G\text{ can be added to }K \text{ in }m\text{ elementary steps}\}. \]

Set \(d(K,G)=0\) when \(G\in K\). A derivation may share previously constructed premises between several branches. Consequently this counts constructions in the hypergraph, including that reuse. For \(K\subseteq L\), and for a single allowed addition \(K'=K\cup\{v\}\),

\[ \boxed{d(L,G)\le d(K,G),\qquad 0\le d(K,G)-d(K',G)\le1.} \]

Proof. Extra knowledge can be ignored, so it cannot increase the minimum. Conversely, prepend the step \(K\to K'\) to a shortest derivation from \(K'\): \(d(K,G)\le1+d(K',G)\).

Thus unit steps partition into those that decrease this distance by one and those that leave it unchanged. Let \(\tau\) count the proposals up to the first proof of \(G\), so \(\tau=\inf\{t:G\in K_t\}\). A successful run satisfies the exact identity

\[ \tau=d(K_0,G)+\#\{\text{steps that leave }d\text{ unchanged}\}. \]

There may be many such steps, even when every construction is valid and retained. This minimum belongs to the fixed hypergraph and step resolution; computing it already requires knowledge of possible proofs. A step that leaves it unchanged can still help discover a route, or serve other targets.

Finite closure bound. If \(\operatorname{cl}(K_0)\) has \(N\) nodes, a process that keeps adding new enabled nodes reaches \(G\) in at most \(N-|K_0|\) additions. Indeed, before \(G\) is known, the first missing step of a derivation of \(G\) supplies an enabled addition. This bounds successful constructions; locating them may require failed proposals.

Equal degrees, different growth

How much does the arrangement of dependencies matter? Take \(N\ge8\) nodes, with \(N\) divisible by four. Include each node in the initial library independently with probability \(\rho\); call these initially known nodes seeds. In each of the following hypergraphs, every node has three incoming binary rules and appears as a premise in six rules.

Groups. Partition the nodes into groups of four. For each node, any pair of the other three can produce it. A group fills exactly when it starts with at least two seeds. A particular node therefore enters the closure if it is a seed itself, or if at least two of its three neighbors are seeds. Its probability of doing so is

\[ x_{\mathrm{groups}}(\rho) =\rho+(1-\rho)(3\rho^2-2\rho^3). \]

Independent groups make the closure fraction converge in probability to this smooth polynomial.

Ring. Index nodes modulo \(N\). For each \(i\), use every pair of \(i-1,i-2,i-3\) as premises for \(i\). Two adjacent seeds propagate around the whole ring. Seeds at distance two first produce an adjacent pair. If no seed pair has cyclic distance one or two, no rule is enabled. Therefore

\[ 1-(1-\rho^2)^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor} \le\Pr(\text{ring fills})\le\min\{1,2N\rho^2\}. \]

Proof of the bounds. The lower bound uses disjoint adjacent pairs, whose seed events are independent. For the upper bound, take the union bound over the \(2N\) pairs at cyclic distance one or two.

The ring fills with probability tending to one if \(N\rho^2\to\infty\), and to zero if \(N\rho^2\to0\). Its threshold scale is \(N^{-1/2}\). For fixed \(0<\rho<1\), the ring's closure fraction tends to one while the groups' fraction stays below one. Uniform step costs, arity, and every degree agree; the arrangement of dependencies changes the large-system behavior.

A random ensemble with a discontinuous transition

We can also ask what happens when the dependencies are chosen randomly. Let \(0<\rho<1\) set the initially known fraction and \(\lambda>0\) the number of rules per node. Choose \(\lfloor\rho N\rfloor\) seeds uniformly among \(N\) labels. Independently, sample \(\lfloor\lambda N\rfloor\) rules \(\{a,b\}\to c\) uniformly, with three distinct labels and with replacement. Let \(X_N=|\operatorname{cl}(K_0)|/N\) be the fraction of nodes eventually obtained. This is an ensemble of finite construction regions; derivations outside the region are omitted.

To guess the limiting fraction \(x\), suppose each prerequisite were independently known with probability \(x\). A binary rule would then be enabled with probability \(x^2\). The number of incoming rules is approximately Poisson with mean \(\lambda\), giving probability \(e^{-\lambda x^2}\) that none is enabled. A node remains unknown if it was not seeded and no rule produces it. This suggests

\[ \boxed{x=1-(1-\rho)e^{-\lambda x^2}.} \]

Known theorem. The independence argument is a heuristic, but a limit theorem justifies its equation. When the smallest solution \(x\) is simple, meaning the two sides have different slopes there, \(X_N\to x\) in probability. This is the positive-closure result in Moore, Istrate, Demopoulos, and Vardi, Lemma 3.1. The equation can have several solutions. Iterating its right-hand side from \(x_0=\rho\) selects the smallest: the iterates increase and are bounded above by every fixed point.

The transition follows by solving for \(\rho\):

\[ \rho(x)=1-(1-x)e^{\lambda x^2},\qquad \rho'(x)=e^{\lambda x^2}[1-2\lambda x(1-x)]. \]

For \(\lambda<2\), this derivative is positive. For \(\lambda>2\), the lower branch ends at a local maximum:

\[ x_- =\frac{1-\sqrt{1-2/\lambda}}2,\qquad \rho_c=1-(1-x_-)e^{\lambda x_-^2}. \]

Increasing \(\rho\) through \(\rho_c\) removes the lower root, so the limiting closure jumps. For \(\lambda=3\), \(\rho_c\approx0.0982574\): the least root is about \(0.19994\) at \(\rho=0.098\), and \(0.93434\) at \(\rho=0.099\).

How much does the final known fraction change when the initially known fraction increases? Differentiating the equation along a simple branch gives

\[ \frac{dx}{d\rho} =\frac{1-x}{(1-\rho)[1-2\lambda x(1-x)]}. \]

This derivative diverges as the lower branch approaches its endpoint. Equal-cost local constructions can therefore exhibit both collective amplification and a singular response to additional initial knowledge.

Details of the limit theorem and its continuous endpoint

The cited theorem uses parameters \((d_1,d_2,d_3)=(\rho,0,\lambda)\). Symmetry allows its excluded seed label to be randomized, giving the uniform seed set used here.

At \(\lambda=2\), \(\rho'(x)\) vanishes only at \(x=1/2\); the root remains unique and continuous. Its seed density is \(1-\sqrt e/2\). To extend the simple-root limit theorem there, couple nested seed sets at \(\rho_c-\varepsilon,\rho_c,\rho_c+\varepsilon\). Closure monotonicity sandwiches the middle fraction between the outer two. First take \(N\to\infty\), then \(\varepsilon\to0\). The outer limits both approach \(1/2\). At a discontinuous endpoint they differ, so this argument gives no concentration claim there. The plotted curves are large-system limits, not observations of an actual prover.

Percolation and finite observations

The closure operation is a generalized bootstrap-percolation process: known nodes are active, and a node activates when one of its prescribed prerequisite sets is active. Its formulation as directed-hypergraph propagation retains the AND condition within each rule. An infinite ambient graph is compatible with every actual exploration and every proof being finite.

For comparison, ordinary bond percolation keeps each edge of a graph independently with probability \(p\). Suppose the graph \(\Gamma\) is infinite, connected, locally finite, and vertex-transitive. Fix a starting vertex \(o\), and let \(B_R\) contain the vertices within distance \(R\) of it. We compare the probability \(q_R(p)\) of an open path leaving this finite ball with the probability \(\theta(p)\) of connection to infinity:

\[ q_R(p)=\Pr_p(o\text{ connects to the boundary of }B_R), \qquad \theta(p)=\Pr_p(o\leftrightarrow\infty). \]

Reaching the boundary means crossing an edge leaving the ball. The critical probability \(p_c=\inf\{p:\theta(p)>0\}\) marks the onset of a positive chance of connection to infinity. The sharpness bounds hold with some \(c=c(p,\Gamma)>0\):

\[ \begin{array}{ll} p<p_c:&q_R(p)\le e^{-cR},\\ p>p_c:&0\le q_R(p)-\theta(p)\le e^{-cR}. \end{array} \]

The supercritical bound is Diskin and collaborators, Theorem 1 (2026); the subcritical bound is classical. These concern complete finite balls of a specified random graph. Applying them to proof hypergraphs would require a corresponding theorem: countability and equal costs do not supply independence, symmetry, or ordinary connectivity. An arbitrary prover trace need not expose a complete ball. Sharpness also does not mean the order parameter must jump discontinuously.

What might a prover exploit?

The graph supplies both obstructions and opportunities: joint prerequisites, shared intermediates, dependency bottlenecks, and regions where new knowledge triggers cascades. A prover must select steps without knowing their full consequences. Humans and autoprovers nevertheless seem to have a sense of direction: we simplify a goal, introduce a construction, or choose a promising lemma before possessing the completed proof.

The distance above gives one precise interpretation. Let \(p_t\) be the conditional probability that the next proposal decreases \(d(K_t,G)\), given the entire run so far. If \(p_t\ge\varepsilon>0\) until success, then

\[ \mathbb E[\tau]\le\frac{d(K_0,G)}{\varepsilon}. \]

Proof. With \(T_n=\min(\tau,n)\), expected accumulated decreases are at least \(\varepsilon\mathbb E[T_n]\) and at most \(d(K_0,G)\). Let \(n\to\infty\). No independence of successive choices is needed. For uniform selection from a finite candidate set, \(p_t\) is simply its fraction of distance-decreasing moves.

Can recognizable features of the hypergraph predict those moves? Does useful direction come from simulating short continuations, learned structural regularities, or both? Can properties measured in explored regions predict cascade thresholds elsewhere? These are research questions; the formulas above do not supply that prediction.

The refrigerator analogy asks for a resource law for reaching a desired state. We have specified a unit of reasoning cost and exhibited structural and statistical laws. Relating them to physical work or a mathematical notion of entropy remains to be done.
